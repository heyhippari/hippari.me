## Why write about writing?

Beginning this blog with an essay about writing might seem a bit obtuse, or even pretentious. Still, I think it matters to establish early why I made certain presentation and structural choices, and how thinking about the ways we communicate can lay the foundations for establishing a voice and a relationship with the readers.

The act of writing itself involves a collection of decisions. Together, these decisions determine who can access the text, how effortlessly they can process the content, and what they might gain from it. Presentation also plays a crucial part in communication. As such, it should also involve a similar amount of care and consideration.

I find it best to make most of these decisions before even deciding *what* to write about, as they shape a lot of what the content will look and feel like, beyond the subject itself.

Through this post, I want to explain why I consciously made certain choices, what these choices imply and how I expect them to affect my writing. This is not a manifesto for the "correct" way to write, nor an attempt at creating a universal writing formula. Rather, it is an attempt to challenge myself to externalize the choices I made while planning this space, and why I made these choices.

My hope is that making these decisions explicit will not only help readers understand why this space looks and reads the way it does, but also encourage them to reflect on the countless visible and invisible choices that shape every text they encounter, and how these choices influence who we include, who we exclude, and how we communicate ideas.

## The myth of neutrality

:::definition
**neutrality** (noun)

*The state or quality of being neutral; the condition of being unengaged in contests between others; state of taking no part on either side.*

--- [Wiktionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/neutrality)
:::

:::definition
**objectivity** (noun)

*The state of being objective, just, unbiased, and not influenced by irrational emotions or personal prejudices.*

--- [Wiktionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/objectivity)
:::

Before we delve into the specific choices I made with regard to writing, I find it important to lay some philosophical groundwork around the concept of neutrality.

The idea of neutrality seems appealing. Not only does it provide a buffer between the supposed neutral party and the subject, it confers an air of wisdom, fairness, and impartiality. The neutral party observes from above the fray, objectively assessing the subject without taking part.

In reality, the pretense of neutrality actually represents a position in itself -- generally a hidden one.

Journalism is one of the fields where neutrality is most frequently invoked. News organizations such as Reuters or the Associated Press are often praised for their neutrality, but the mere presence of a human element in the chain from the event taking place to a reader consuming the resulting report -- and, really, a collaboration of different human elements, from the journalist to the editors, the design team of the agency's website, etc -- introduces an element of partiality.

For you to see a piece of information, not only does someone have to know about it, but they also have to deem it important enough to report it. That act of selection, in itself, challenges the idea of neutrality: the journalist acts not as a mere observer, but someone who deems a piece of information important enough to invest their time in sharing it.

Furthermore, every part of the reporting process after the selection of the information introduces further selection. Journalists, while they follow style guides for the publication they write for and adhere to journalistic standards, have to choose which details they include in their reporting and what context they find relevant to include around the event.

The articles also feature a choice of language, both in the content of the piece but also in its headline, which has to be carefully balanced between conveying information, attracting the readers, and leaving enough information aside for the main reporting. The place on a website or newsprint that an article occupies also reflects many implicit or explicit decisions about importance and visibility.

The neutrality of a piece of reporting, then, only exists within a framework of assumptions and rules that already exists before the reporting takes place. These rules, often unspoken and unwritten, tend to feel axiomatic and unshakeable.

:::aside
"*When you have to make a choice and don't make it, that is in itself a choice.*"

--- Generally attributed to [William James](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_James), philosopher and psychologist
:::

Yet, the pretense of neutrality does not dissolve those rules, it moves them out of view. By presenting those assumptions as the natural or neutral baseline, rather than as choices made within a specific context, neutrality can unintentionally reinforce existing conventions and ways of thinking.

Writing is subject to the same limitations. A text cannot exist outside the choices of its author: what they include, what they omit, what language they use, and what knowledge they assume. A writer may attempt to be neutral, but even the decision of what deserves an explanation and what can be left unsaid creates a relationship with the reader. In this sense, writing is not only an act of transmitting information, but also an act of defining the conditions under which that information is received.
